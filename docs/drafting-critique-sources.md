# Drafting manuals and courts expose uncatalogued weaknesses

The library of 188 weakness patterns is complete against its own two source lists and incomplete against the wider literature. Every one of the 226 headings in the two lists appears in some pattern, yet the official drafting manuals, the canons courts apply, oversight reports, the avoidance scholarship and press critiques together yield **133 candidate patterns in eight clusters** that the library does not hold. The largest holes are sentence mechanics (modifier scope, "and" versus "or", punctuation, tense, time computation), amendatory mechanics, restructuring by the regulated party, and what happens when the executive declines to act. Congress does publish drafting standards: the House Office of the Legislative Counsel (HOLC) issues a Manual on Drafting Style (2022 edition) and three shorter guides, the Senate office has a 1997 manual, and Title 1 of the U.S. Code sets default rules of construction, but none of the manuals is binding and none addresses enforcement design, preemption or penalties. Nine fixes in the library sit in tension with those manuals or with law as it stands in November 2026, most plainly the advice to add a general severability clause, to grant standing to organizations by statute, and to rely on agency penalties and independent enforcers. **114 of the 188 patterns are tagged as AI-origin**, so several ideas that apply to any bill are worded so that they fire only on AI bills. The source base is balanced among nonpartisan and libertarian critics and thinner on the right, where several outlets blocked retrieval; that limit is stated in detail below.

## Congress publishes drafting standards, and none of them binds

### The House manual is a style code that discourages findings, purposes and boilerplate

HOLC publishes four public documents: the [House Legislative Counsel's Manual on Drafting Style](https://legcounsel.house.gov/sites/evo-subsites/legcounsel-evo.house.gov/files/documents/ManualDraftStyle_2022.pdf) (2022 edition, the successor to the 1995 manual), a web [Guide to Legislative Drafting](https://legcounsel.house.gov/holc-guide-legislative-drafting), a [Quick Guide](https://legcounsel.house.gov/sites/evo-subsites/legcounsel-evo.house.gov/files/evo-media-document/quick_guide_6_10_26.pdf) and an [Introduction to Legislative Drafting](https://legcounsel.house.gov/sites/evo-subsites/legcounsel-evo.house.gov/files/evo-media-document/intro_to_drafting_6_10_26.pdf), the last two revised 10 June 2026. They are office conventions, "drafted by OLC, not enacted by Congress" in the words of a [Yale Law Journal note](https://www.yalelawjournal.org/pdf/909_v5egw6x5.pdf), which also found that federal courts had relied on the manuals only three times.

The manual's rules are mostly about form. It asks for the present tense, the singular, the active voice "unless the actor cannot be identified", "may" for a power and "shall" for a duty, and "may not" as the modern form of prohibition. It reserves "means" for complete definitions and "includes" for partial ones and calls "but is not limited to" redundant. It calls provisos archaic, prefers "not later than" to "within", tells drafters to use the same word for the same thing throughout, and orders a section as general rule, exceptions, special rules, transitional rules, other provisions. It also sets rules for amendatory instructions and notes that repealing an amending law does not restore the earlier text. Three of its positions bear directly on the library: section 325 says "Discourage clients from including findings and purposes"; section 328 says a severability clause "is unnecessary unless it provides in detail" which provisions fall; and section 327 calls "such sums as may be necessary" unnecessary. The manual has no section on sense of Congress provisions, "and/or", savings clauses or "notwithstanding any other provision of law".

The Introduction contains the closest thing HOLC offers to a substantive checklist, a list of drafter's questions: to whom the policy applies, what exceptions exist, who carries it out, whether states or the federal government are responsible, "What if the policy is not followed?", whether penalties are civil or criminal, how much lead time is needed, whether transitional rules are required, and whether existing law must be amended to avoid conflict. The library already tracks most of these; missing transitional rules is the exception.

### The Senate manual goes further on logic, deadlines and conflicts with other law

The Senate Office of the Legislative Counsel's [Legislative Drafting Manual](https://law.yale.edu/sites/default/files/documents/pdf/Faculty/SenateOfficeoftheLegislativeCounsel_LegislativeDraftingManual%281997%29.pdf) dates from February 1997, has never been officially published by the Senate, and is public through a copy hosted by Yale Law School. Where it overlaps the House manual the two agree. It adds rules the House manual lacks. Section 302 governs "and" and "or" and says "'And/or' has not gained acceptance in legislative drafting." Section 325 rejects "within days of" as ambiguous and notes that "before" and "after" leave the named date uncovered. Section 314 says "Avoid the use of 'notwithstanding any other provision of law'", tells the drafter to identify and cite the conflicting law, and warns that the phrase does not override a treaty. Section 131 says a severability clause "is unnecessary" and supplies a form for a nonseverability clause instead. Section 127 notes that a sunset of authority does not repeal amendments the Act made, and section 130 that an authorization "does not create authority to obligate the amount provided". Section 105 states the reason consistency matters: "A court presumes that different words have different meanings." Neither federal manual gives rules for preemption clauses, private rights of action, penalties or enforcement.

### Statute, chamber rules and executive directives fill in around the manuals

[1 U.S.C. 1](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/1/1) supplies default meanings "unless the context indicates otherwise": singular includes plural, the present tense includes the future, and "person" includes corporations and other entities. [Chapter 2 of Title 1](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?path=/prelim@title1/chapter2&edition=prelim) requires that each section "contain, as nearly as may be, a single proposition of enactment", provides that a repealed Act is not revived by repeal of the repealer "unless it shall be expressly so provided", and saves penalties and liabilities incurred under a repealed statute. The [Office of the Law Revision Counsel](https://uscode.house.gov/codification/legislation.shtml) explains positive law codification, which matters to drafters because a positive law title is amended directly while a non-positive title is amended through the underlying Act.

Chamber rules constrain content and accompanying documents without governing style. The House Ramseyer rule requires a committee report to print the text of each section to be repealed and "a comparative print of each amendment" ([House Rules and Manual, 118th Congress](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/HMAN-118/html/HMAN-118-pg662.htm)). The [Standing Rules of the Senate](https://www.rules.senate.gov/download/the-rules-of-the-senate-117th) contain the matching Cordon rule, require a cost estimate and a regulatory impact evaluation in committee reports, bar general legislation on appropriations bills and confine conferees to matter committed to them. The Byrd rule treats a reconciliation provision as extraneous on six grounds, including one that "does not produce a change in outlays or revenues" ([CRS RL30862](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/RL/PDF/RL30862/RL30862.16.pdf)). All are enforced by points of order and can be waived.

On the executive side, [OMB Circular A-19](https://www.whitehouse.gov/wp-content/uploads/2017/11/Circular-019.pdf) (1979) requires agency proposals to carry a section-by-section analysis and budget and personnel impacts, and limits backdoor financing. The [Plain Writing Act of 2010](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/PLAW-111publ274/html/PLAW-111publ274.htm) covers executive agency documents only, excludes regulations and bars judicial review of compliance, so it does not reach bills. The [Document Drafting Handbook](https://www.archives.gov/federal-register/write/handbook) governs Federal Register documents. Executive Order 12988, as described in [CRS Report 97-589](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/20111219_97-589_ecc097f4f874b4d55b934f53cbb035432bfba150.pdf), directs executive drafters to state limitations periods, preemptive effect, retroactivity, burdens of proof and key definitions in clear language; the library tracks each of those items.

Guides outside the federal government state rules the federal manuals lack. The Uniform Law Commission's drafting rules, quoted in a [Multistate Tax Commission training deck](https://www.mtc.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/02/Drafting-Rules-for-Uniform-Statutes-Regulations.pdf), say "Do NOT use 'shall' or 'must' to state a rule of law" and "Do not include substantive provisions in a definition." The UK [Office of the Parliamentary Counsel guidance](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/660407d091a320001a82b06b/2024.03.19.Drafting-guidance.pdf) avoids the legislative "shall" altogether, warns against "sandwich" provisions and mixed conjunctions, and tells drafters to add "or both" where "or" is inclusive. The EU [Joint Practical Guide](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/content/techleg/EN-legislative-drafting-guide.pdf) bars circular and serial references, requires a static reference to say so, and bars autonomous substantive provisions inside amending acts. Australia's [Drafting Directions](https://www.opc.gov.au/drafting-resources/drafting-directions) add subject-specific directions on offences, regulatory powers, evidence and conferral of powers, a layer the federal manuals lack entirely; only their titles and dates were confirmed.

## Five shelves of critique, each teaching a different lesson

### Textbooks and treatises teach how a court will read the sentence

The standard drafting references are Filson and Strokoff's Legislative Drafter's Desk Reference (second edition 2008, per a [Berkeley Law catalog record](https://lawcat.berkeley.edu/record/209871); a third edition is listed for 2024), Reed Dickerson's work, Xanthaki's Drafting Legislation and her continuation of [Thornton's Legislative Drafting](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10178134/1/Xanthaki_2022%20Thornton%20Second%20proofs%20%282%29.pdf), Garner's Guidelines for Drafting and Editing Legislation, and Martineau's Plain English for Drafting Statutes and Rules. The interpretation shelf is Sutherland, Scalia and Garner's Reading Law (57 canons, per a [Claremont review](https://claremontreviewofbooks.com/taking-law-seriously/)), Eskridge's Interpreting Law, Bennion, Katzmann and Cross, and the casebooks by Eskridge and co-authors, Jellum, and Ginsburg and Louk. Only tables of contents, abstracts, catalog records and reviews of these books were opened; no book was read in full.

Two taxonomies transfer directly. Dickerson's [1964 article](https://www.repository.law.indiana.edu/facpub/1507) names five "diseases" of legislative language: ambiguity, over-vagueness, over-precision, over-generality and under-generality, and obesity. Layman Allen's [chapter](https://core.ac.uk/download/232688227.pdf) splits ambiguity into semantic and syntactic and gives quantifier scope, placement of negation and the connective "unless" as worked examples. The most neutral single canon list opened is the appendix to [CRS Report R45153](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/R45153.html), which names 33 semantic and 23 substantive canons. Reviewers of the two leading canon books argue that Eskridge's list is too long and Scalia and Garner's too selective, and that canons come in opposing pairs ([Krishnakumar and Nourse](https://texaslawreview.org/the-canon-wars/)).

The lesson for the library is that it describes how a policy is weakened and the books describe how a sentence is read. A keyword search of the library returns nothing for lenity, surplusage, antecedent, series qualifier, proviso, punctuation, mens rea or scrivener. A public [Vermont Law syllabus](https://www.vermontlaw.edu/sites/default/files/2023-05/SYLSU23_LegislationRegulation_Fregosi.pdf) gives about five of eighteen sessions to text-level canons, the area where the library is thinnest. One caution cuts the other way: a [survey of 137 congressional staffers](https://www.stanfordlawreview.org/print/article/statutory-interpretation-from-the-inside-an-empirical-study-of-congressional-drafting-delegation-and-the-canons-part-i/) found that drafters know but consciously reject the presumption of consistent usage and the rule against superfluities, so a tool that applies those canons will flag text the drafters considered fine while still predicting how a court may read it.

### Scholarship on avoidance, capture and implementation describes the response to the text

The avoidance literature describes one move at several levels of generality. Fleischer defines regulatory arbitrage as exploiting "a gap between the economics of a deal and its regulatory treatment" ([abstract](https://taxprofblog.aals.org/2010/03/11/fleischer-12/)). McBarnet's [creative compliance](https://openresearch-repository.anu.edu.au/bitstream/1885/154916/1/18.pdf) is repackaging activity so that it "can be defended as not non-compliance" while defeating the policy. Kamin and twelve co-authors sorted the problems of the 2017 tax law into games, roadblocks and glitches and named specific games such as "cracking" a business into entities and "packing" favored activity into a mixed one ([103 Minn. L. Rev. 1439](https://core.ac.uk/download/269679691.pdf)). That three-way split works as a triage for any bill: what the text lets a planner do, what other legal regimes it collides with, and where it contains an error.

On the design side, Coglianese, Scheffler and Walters count roughly one obligation-alleviating word for every five to six obligation-imposing words in federal law and report that for most dispensation provisions they "were unable to find information about how often and for whom dispensations were granted" ([Unrules](https://www.stanfordlawreview.org/print/article/unrules/)). Gluck, O'Connell and Po catalogue [unorthodox lawmaking](https://www.columbialawreview.org/?p=395): omnibus and emergency bills, committee bypass, outside drafters and automatic procedures. Kysar shows sunsets used to fit budget rules ([Dynamic Legislation](https://core.ac.uk/download/228310639.pdf)). Nash names [null preemption](https://ndlawreview.org/wp-content/uploads/2013/07/Nash.pdf), where state law is displaced and the federal level is set at zero. Carpenter and Moss supply a standard that supports neutral description: a capture claim requires a defeasible model of the public interest, a shift away from it, and industry action sufficient to cause the shift, so benefit alone is not evidence ([Preventing Regulatory Capture](https://www.tobinproject.org/sites/default/files/assets/Introduction%20from%20Preventing%20Regulatory%20Capture.pdf)). Several of these authors defend the features they describe: Barron and Rakoff defend broad waiver, and Kysar treats sunsets as an occasion for review. A pattern drawn from this shelf should carry reasons for and against.

The AI governance literature of 2022 to 2026 mostly finds the same mechanisms again. The clearest addition is self-determined coverage: a field study of New York City Local Law 144 found that of 391 employers, 18 had posted bias audits, and that because employers decide whether their tool is in scope, silence cannot be told apart from non-compliance ([Wright et al.](https://www.arxiv.org/abs/2406.01399)).

### Oversight bodies document structure, and courts document single words

The Government Accountability Office addresses "matters for congressional consideration" to Congress when agency action cannot fix a problem; **277 were open as of 9 April 2026** ([GAO-26-108896](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-26-108896)). The defects GAO reports are structural: FirstNet's authority sunsets in 2027 with no successor named for its contract and fee stream ([GAO-22-104915](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-22-104915)); Medicare pays more for the same service by site and the agency "lacks the statutory authority" to equalize it ([GAO-16-189](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-16-189)); pandemic unemployment eligibility rested on self-certification ([DOL OIG](https://www.oversight.gov/sites/default/files/oig-reports/19-21-001-03-315_COVID%2019%20Self%20Cert_Final%20Rpt_102120.pdf)). Inspector general reports are most useful for the lawful but unintended use: the State Department's inspector general found a 2019 emergency arms certification "executed in accordance with the requirements" of the Act and also reported 4,221 below-threshold transfers that the Act does not require be notified to Congress ([ISP-I-20-19](https://www.oversight.gov/sites/default/files/documents/reports/2020-08/ISP-I-20-19.pdf)).

The federal judiciary runs a project that sends Congress appellate opinions noting "missing or misleading punctuation, grammatical problems affecting meaning, ambiguous language, or gaps in time periods" ([U.S. Courts](https://www.uscourts.gov/data-news/judiciary-news/2015/09/10/project-statutory-construction-promotes-inter-branch-communications)). Of 52 opinions sent from 2007 to 2015, 35 were classed as ambiguity and 16 as gaps. A later study counted 61 opinions through 2019 and concluded that Congress "specifically addressed two" ([Levy and Narechania](https://www.californialawreview.org/print/interbranch-information-sharing-examining-the-statutory-opinion-transmission-project)). The Administrative Conference ran a parallel pilot from 2018 and ended it in 2022 for a "paucity of relevant cases" ([ACUS](https://acus.gov/node/3395)). Voluntary referral channels produce little, so the corpus of unfixed defects is the opinions themselves.

Supreme Court decisions show how small the decisive text can be. The same sentence shape, a modifier after a list, was resolved by the last antecedent rule in [Lockhart](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/14-8358) and by the series qualifier rule in [Facebook v. Duguid](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/19-511) five years later. [Pulsifer](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/22-340) turned on "and" in a negated list, [Niz-Chavez](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/19-863) on the article "a", [Van Buren](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/19-783) on the word "so". In [King v. Burwell](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/14-114) the Court wrote that "The Affordable Care Act contains more than a few examples of inartful drafting." A checker that examines only defined terms and operative verbs misses all of these.

### Outlets and institutes report by beat, and by orientation

No publication dedicated to legislative loopholes as such was found. Text-level reporting concentrates in tax (ProPublica, Bloomberg Tax), surveillance and technology (Reason, 404 Media, Lawfare, Just Security), health (KFF Health News) and congressional ethics (The Lever, Sludge). Provision-level critiques were opened from 22 organizations: on the left or progressive side the Brennan Center, Americans for Financial Reform, Better Markets, the Center on Budget and Policy Priorities, the Institute on Taxation and Economic Policy, the Electronic Frontier Foundation, the Center for Democracy and Technology and the Electronic Privacy Information Center; on the right the American Enterprise Institute, the American Action Forum, the National Taxpayers Union Foundation and the Oversight Project; libertarian or market-oriented, the Cato Institute, the Competitive Enterprise Institute, R Street, the Tax Foundation and the Niskanen Center; and nonpartisan or cross-partisan, the Campaign Legal Center, the Project On Government Oversight, the Committee for a Responsible Federal Budget, Taxpayers for Common Sense and the Bipartisan Policy Center. Orientation labels here are shorthand from general knowledge and were not sourced to an opened page.

The orientations pick different slices of the same drafting problems. Left-leaning sources report industry carve-outs, weak ethics rules and tax avoidance. Right-leaning sources report budget presentation devices, eligibility gaps and executive discretion. Libertarian sources report vagueness and overbreadth in speech and surveillance law. That last slice matters for the library, which treats under-coverage as the typical weakness and has no general pattern for a definition that sweeps in bystanders. Reason's account of the 2024 surveillance reauthorization, where a provider definition was said to reach landlords and cleaning contractors and was limited only by an Attorney General's letter, is the model case ([Reason](https://reason.com/2024/04/19/how-the-fisa-reauthorization-bill-could-force-maintenance-workers-and-custodians-to-become-government-spies/)).

The word "loophole" is itself contested. The National Taxpayers Union Foundation argues that a ProPublica story mislabeled amortization, a deliberate and well-known rule, as a loophole ([NTU Foundation](https://www.ntu.org/foundation/detail/propublica-doesnt-get-the-tax-code-so-stirs-up-non-existent-problems)). A catalogue that aims at neutrality should record for each instance whether the gap was unintended, intended or disputed. Fact-checkers are a good source for mismatches between a bill's marketing and its text because they quote the operative words beside the claim, as [FactCheck.org](https://www.factcheck.org/2025/02/will-save-act-prevent-married-women-from-registering-to-vote/) did with the SAVE Act. Law firm and accounting alerts record, in near real time, which exceptions regulated parties intend to use and when an agency closes or confirms a route.

### Where balance was and was not achieved

Balance was achieved among institutes: eight left or progressive, five right-leaning counting Heritage, five libertarian or market-oriented and five nonpartisan organizations were identified, and all but Heritage have at least one opened example. Balance was not achieved in four places. First, right-leaning news outlets are represented by titles only, because National Review and The Dispatch refused retrieval and the Washington Examiner piece was paywalled; the Heritage Foundation's reports also failed to load. Second, no example was opened for Public Citizen, CREW, the ACLU or the Mercatus Center, so gaps exist on both sides. Third, the enforcement design literature that was opened (Burbank, Farhang and Kritzer; Lemos; Adelman and Glicksman; an environmental justice center's citizen suit review) is on the whole favorable to private and state enforcement; the opposing considerations in this report come from CRS and ACUS, not from scholars critical of private enforcement. Fourth, the only essay opened on drafting delegations after Loper Bright is from a single think tank, the Foundation for American Innovation, and is advocacy. The opened examples are also skewed toward tax, surveillance, technology and ethics law; environmental, labor, immigration and defense procurement statutes are underrepresented.

## 133 candidate patterns in eight clusters are missing from the library

The library was read in full for this analysis (fields: name, plain, fix, cues). "Missing" means no pattern names the mechanism; several broad patterns (dual-reading, constructive-ambiguity, litigation-friendly-drafting) could be read as umbrellas over some rows. Every source linked in the tables below was opened unless the row says otherwise; for some scholarly works only an abstract or a third-party summary was read, as the index further down records. Names are neutral descriptions of what the text permits and carry no judgment about intent.

## Nine library fixes sit in tension with the manuals or current law

**Severability.** The fix for severability-strategy says "Include a clear severability clause." Both manuals call a general clause unnecessary because courts presume severability; the House manual calls it boilerplate. The position consistent with the manuals is to decide which provisions form a package and write a specific nonseverability clause or a fallback for them. The fix for litigation-friendly-drafting ("add severability") carries the same tension.

**Lists.** The lists-and-canons pattern uses "including but not limited to" as a cue and recommends adding "a rule that lists are illustrative." Both manuals say "includes" already signals a partial list, that "but is not limited to" is redundant, and (Senate section 316) that it does not displace ejusdem generis. The risk the manuals identify is "means" used where an open list was intended.

**Findings and purposes.** The fixes for findings-for-deference ("Include specific findings"), major-questions-trigger ("specific findings on scope") and aspirational-preamble ("state that purposes guide interpretation") run against HOLC's instruction to discourage findings and purposes. The conflict is partial: HOLC's own Introduction accepts findings that help establish congressional power, the Senate manual accepts them where they aid clarity or constitutional support, and an enacted rule of construction exists in the [ADA](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/42/12102).

**Standing.** The fix for standing-ambiguity says "allow community and organization standing." Under TransUnion v. Ramirez as described by [CRS](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/LSB10629.html), Congress may not relieve federal courts of deciding whether a plaintiff has a concrete harm, so a statutory grant of standing does not by itself open the federal courthouse.

**Penalties and private rights.** The fix for weak-enforcement ("Add meaningful penalties, enforcement funding and a private right of action") omits three limits documented by CRS: civil penalties imposed in an agency's own tribunal face the jury-trial holding in SEC v. Jarkesy ([LSB11229](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/LSB11229.html)); statutory damages without concrete harm face TransUnion; and rights under spending statutes require "rights-creating terms" after Medina ([LSB11351](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/LSB11351.html)). The same applies to private-right-ambiguity.

**Independent enforcers.** Fixes that rely on an independent regulator or assessor (choosing-regulator, who-conducts-assessment) assume insulation from removal. CRS reports that Trump v. Slaughter (29 June 2026) overruled Humphrey's Executor and held FTC commissioners removable at will, with Federal Reserve governors treated separately ([LSB11448](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/LSB11448.html)). The remaining structural tools CRS lists are Senate confirmation, quorum rules and supermajority voting, each with a cost in slower action.

**Dedicated funding.** The fixes for defunding-without-repealing ("Provide a dedicated funding source") and authorization-vs-appropriation ("Pair authorizations with mandatory funding") are sound as far as they go, and the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau episode shows their limit: the 2025 law cut the Bureau's funding cap from 12 to 6.5 percent ([12 U.S.C. 5497](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/12/5497)), and the Bureau then declined to request funds until a district court ruled against that position ([Cooley](https://finsights.cooley.com/judge-rules-cfpbs-refusal-to-request-funding-violates-preliminary-injunction/)). OMB Circular A-19 and the two manuals also disagree with each other: A-19 calls for "such sums as may be necessary" in later years while both manuals call that phrase unnecessary.

**Deadlines.** The fix for phantom-deadlines recommends a default rule or a right to sue. CRS describes such hammers as "self-enforcing" and also warns of "hastily imposed rules"; it reports a GAO finding that EPA missed 256 of 338 Clean Air Act deadlines ([CRS R45336](https://sgp.fas.org/crs/misc/R45336.pdf)). ACUS Recommendation 78-3 says "Congress ordinarily should not impose statutory time limits on rulemaking proceedings." The library treats weak deadlines as a weakness and has nothing on over-rigid ones.

**Open-textured standards.** The vagueness-and-delegation pattern treats "reasonable" or "appropriate" as a weakness, and the chevron-vacuum fix says to "give express discretion to the agency." Loper Bright itself recognizes that a statute may "expressly delegate" definitional authority or use a term that "leaves agencies with flexibility" ([opinion](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/22-451)), and the opinion cites enacted formulas such as "as such terms are defined and delimited by regulations of the Secretary" ([29 U.S.C. 213](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/29/213)). Flexible terms paired with an express delegation are therefore a recognized drafting choice, and the fix text can name the formula.

Two smaller points follow from the same sources. The sunset-clauses pattern treats a sunset only as a route to ending a program, while Kysar, the Texas Sunset Advisory Commission and a 2026 proposal to attach sunsets to every delegation treat it as a review device ([FAI](https://www.thefai.org/posts/congress-could-just-say-what-it-delegates)); the Senate manual adds that a sunset of authority leaves the Act's amendments in force. The shall-may-ambiguity fix is consistent with both manuals and omits their rule that "may not" is the preferred form of prohibition. The notwithstanding-clause fix matches Senate section 314 and can add that a general override does not reach treaties.

## Conclusion

The two source lists and the library built from them describe legislation the way a policy strategist sees it: who gains from a qualifier, a delay or a carve-out. The official manuals, the canon lists and the appellate record describe it the way a drafter and a judge see it, one sentence at a time, and the oversight record describes it the way an auditor sees it, as a program that does or does not run. The library is strong in the first view and nearly empty in the second and third. Adding a sentence-mechanics family and a program-design family would change what the tool can detect from bill text alone, since modifier scope, conjunctions under negation, time words and amendatory references are mechanically visible in a way that capture and intent are not.

The second shift concerns the fixes more than the weaknesses. The opened record from 2024 to 2026 shows hardened provisions failing at the point where an executive actor had to prosecute, keep coverage in place, request funds or obligate them, and shows several standard remedies meeting new constitutional limits. A useful test for every fix in the library is therefore the same question: who must act for this to bite, and what happens if that actor declines. Recording each pattern with its documented benefits, its failure modes and whether a given instance was intended, unintended or disputed would also bring the library into line with what the literature on both sides of the political spectrum says about itself, which is that showing who benefits from a provision is not the same as showing a defect.
