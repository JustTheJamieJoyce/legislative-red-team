# Conflict of interest review

A measure can fail its own purpose when the person it puts in charge has a tie to the people the measure is about. This review starts with the people: who holds each deciding job today, and what documented ties that person has. It then checks offices and institutions.

Each conflict is stated directly and names the person. A tie is reported as its source states it. The review does not say that any person acted wrongly.

## Order of work

- **People first.** Name who holds each deciding job today. Research that person's documented ties to anyone the measure affects: past clients and employers, who appointed them, money and family. State the tie directly and give its source.
- **Offices.** Check who can appoint or remove the decision-maker, and whether that person is affected by the decisions.
- **Institutions.** Check whether a body decides matters about its own conduct, records or money. This conflict exists whoever holds the office.

## Jobs to look for in the text

- Who must carry out the main duty?
- Who decides when an exception applies?
- Who decides what an undefined word means?
- Who declares that the work is finished?
- Who writes the report, and who receives it?
- Who reviews or audits the decisions?
- Who can enforce the measure if a duty is not met?
- Who pays for the work, and where does the money come from?
- Who appoints and can remove each of those people?
- Whose conduct, records or money is the measure about?
- Who benefits if the measure is carried out weakly?

## Ties to look for

- The holder of the job and the subject of the measure are the same person or body.
- The holder reports to the subject, or can be removed by the subject.
- The holder is paid by the subject, or the holder's budget depends on the result.
- The holder has worked for the subject, or expects to work for the subject later.
- The holder, or the holder's family, has money at stake in the result.
- The holder controls the information that anyone checking the holder's work would need.

## Safeguards to look for

- A decision-maker outside the body whose conduct is at issue.
- A rule that requires an official with a tie to step aside, and names who decides instead.
- Review of the decisions by a court, an inspector general or an independent board.
- A deadline or trigger that does not depend on the holder declaring the work finished.
- A public report, or a report to a body other than the one being reviewed.
- Separate bodies for jobs that pull in opposite directions.
- Protection against removal for the person who reviews the decisions.
- Funding that does not rise or fall with the decisions the holder makes.
- Required disclosure of the holder's ties.
- A second way to enforce the measure, such as a right to sue.

## Types of conflict

### The party judges its own compliance

The same person or body carries out a duty and also decides whether the duty was met. Examples are a party that certifies its own work, declares its own task complete or writes the only report on its own conduct.

What to check: Find every certification, completion date and report in the measure. Ask who produces each one and whether that party is also the one whose performance it describes.

Safeguards: Look for an outside reviewer, an audit, or a deadline that does not depend on the party's own declaration.

Documented example: A House committee report in March 2020 found a failure of oversight by the Federal Aviation Administration in certifying the Boeing 737 MAX. Under a program that critics said created a conflict of interest, the agency had delegated some certification work to Boeing employees. [ABC News, March 6, 2020](https://www.goodmorningamerica.com/news/story/house-faults-boeing-faa-737-max-investigation-69441764)

### The party must investigate or disclose its own conduct

A measure orders a body to investigate, release or report on matters that include that body's own past decisions, its leaders, or the people who appoint its leaders.

What to check: List what the measure tells the body to release or investigate. Ask whether any of it documents the body's own conduct or the conduct of people the body answers to.

Safeguards: Look for an independent decision-maker, a recusal rule, or outside review of what is withheld.

Documented example: Congress created independent counsels in the Ethics in Government Act of 1978 because conflicts of interest can arise when the executive branch investigates its own officials. Congress let those provisions lapse in 1999. [Congressional Research Service, Special Counsel Investigations (R44857)](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/R44857.html)

### The holder depends on someone affected by the decision

The official given the job is appointed by, reports to, or can be removed by a person whose interests the decision affects.

What to check: For each job in the measure, find who appoints and who can remove the holder. Ask whether that person is affected by the decisions the holder must make.

Safeguards: Look for a fixed term, limits on removal, or a decision-maker outside that chain of command.

Documented example: The Inspector General Act lets the President remove most inspectors general after giving Congress 30 days' written notice of the reasons. The statute does not limit the grounds for removal, so their independence rests largely on deterrence and congressional oversight. [Congressional Research Service, Presidential Removal of IGs (LSB10476)](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/LSB10476.html)

### The current holder has a tie to an affected person

The person who holds the job now has worked for, represented, funded or been funded by someone the measure affects. This type depends on who holds the office, so it can change when the officeholder changes.

What to check: Identify who currently holds each job. Research that person's past employers, clients and public roles, and compare them with the people and bodies the measure affects. Report a tie only with a source.

Safeguards: Look for a recusal rule and for a named substitute who decides when the holder steps aside.

### The decision-maker has money at stake

The official who decides, or that official's family, would gain or lose money depending on the decision.

What to check: Ask whether the measure lets an official decide matters that affect companies or assets the official may own, and whether it relies on general ethics law or sets its own rule.

Safeguards: Look for disclosure, divestment or recusal requirements written into the measure.

Documented example: A federal criminal statute, section 208 of title 18 of the United States Code, bars executive branch employees from taking part in matters that affect their own financial interests. [18 U.S.C. 208](https://uscode.house.gov/us/usc/119-121/t18/s208/)

### The deciding body is funded by its own decisions

The body that decides keeps the fines, fees or property that result from its decisions, or is funded by the parties it oversees.

What to check: Trace where fines, fees and forfeited property go, and who pays for the body's budget. Ask whether the body's income rises when it decides one way.

Safeguards: Look for money that goes to a general fund, and for a budget that does not depend on the parties being overseen.

Documented example: In Tumey v. Ohio (1927) the Supreme Court held that it violated due process for a judge to be paid from the fines imposed on the defendants he convicted. Separately, fees paid by drug companies now fund more than 65 percent of the Food and Drug Administration's human drugs program. Supporters cite faster reviews, and critics say the fees give industry undue influence. [Constitution Annotated: Unbiased Judge](https://law.cornell.edu/constitution-conan/amendment-5/unbiased-judge) and [Pharmacy Times on the Prescription Drug User Fee Act](https://www.pharmacytimes.com/view/the-prescription-drug-user-fee-act-good-bad-or-a-lot-of-both-)

### One body must both promote and police the same activity

A single agency is told to encourage an industry, or collect revenue from it, and also to enforce safety or other limits on it.

What to check: List every mission the measure gives to one body. Ask whether success at one mission is made harder by strict performance of another.

Safeguards: Look for separate bodies, or separate leadership and budgets, for missions that pull in opposite directions.

Documented example: After the Deepwater Horizon oil spill, the Interior Department said the Minerals Management Service had inherently conflicted missions: resource management, safety oversight and revenue collection. The Department divided those missions among three separate offices in 2010 and 2011. [Department of the Interior](https://www.doi.gov/node/14124)

### Regulated parties do the regulator's work

The measure lets the parties being regulated write the standards, sit on the deciding body, or certify their own products or conduct.

What to check: Find every place the measure relies on industry standards, industry members of a board, or certification by the regulated party. Ask who checks that work.

Safeguards: Look for a public agency that must approve the standard or the certification, and for limits on how many seats regulated parties hold.

### The examiner is chosen or paid by the party it examines

An auditor, rater, inspector or evaluator is selected or paid by the party whose work it must judge.

What to check: For each audit, rating, inspection or evaluation in the measure, ask who selects the examiner and who pays.

Safeguards: Look for assignment of the examiner by a public body, for rotation, and for payment that does not come from the party examined.

### The party that shapes the rules can then compete under them

A contractor or grantee helps design a program, write its requirements or evaluate its results, and can then seek the contract or grant.

What to check: Ask whether the measure lets a party advise on requirements, standards or awards and also receive the resulting contracts or grants.

Safeguards: Look for a rule that bars the advisor from competing, or that requires the conflict to be disclosed and reduced.

Documented example: Federal procurement rules call this an organizational conflict of interest. They cover a contractor that helps set the terms of a competition it can then enter, and a contractor whose other interests could impair its objectivity. [Congressional Research Service, Organizational Conflicts of Interest (LSB10772)](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/LSB10772.html)

### The body that writes the rule exempts itself

The measure applies to others and leaves out the legislature, the executive office or the agency that wrote it.

What to check: Read the definitions and exceptions for any wording that leaves out Congress, the President, the courts or the agency in charge. Ask whether the reason given applies equally to those left in.

Safeguards: Look for wording that applies the rule to the body itself, or a separate office that applies it there.

Documented example: Congress exempted itself from federal labor laws such as the Fair Labor Standards Act of 1938 when it passed them. The Congressional Accountability Act of 1995 applied many of those laws to Congress for the first time. [Roll Call, January 28, 2025](https://rollcall.com/2025/01/28/living-under-laws-they-make-congressional-accountability-act/)

### The overseen party controls the information the overseer needs

The body being checked decides what information the checker receives, for example by classifying records, by self-reporting data or by deciding what counts as responsive.

What to check: Ask what an overseer would need in order to test compliance, and who holds it. Ask whether the measure lets the overseen party withhold, summarize or classify that information.

Safeguards: Look for a right of direct access to records, and for review of withholding by someone outside the body.

### The only enforcer has a reason not to enforce

The measure can be enforced only by a body that is itself bound by the measure, or that is closely tied to the party that is bound.

What to check: List everyone who can enforce the measure. Ask whether each one is independent of the party that owes the duty, and whether anyone else can act if that enforcer does not.

Safeguards: Look for a second enforcer, such as a right to sue, state attorneys general or an inspector general.

### Decision-makers move between the regulator and the regulated

The measure creates decisions of high value to an industry and sets no limit on officials who come from that industry or leave to work for it.

What to check: Ask whether the measure creates new approvals, awards or enforcement choices, and whether it sets any waiting period before an official may work for an affected party.

Safeguards: Look for waiting periods, disclosure of job negotiations and limits on appointing recent industry employees.
