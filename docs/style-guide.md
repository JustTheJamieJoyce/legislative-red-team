# Writing style guide

Every line the tool shows is written to this guide and checked against it.

## The test

The stranger test: imagine a person who has never heard of this measure and sees only this one piece of text, with nothing else on the screen. They must be able to tell a friend what it says. If they would have to ask "which law?", "records about what?", "which branch?", "what exception?" or "what does that word mean?", the text fails.

## Rules for all text

- Every title, explanation, reason and question is one or more complete sentences. No headline shorthand, no sentence fragments, no dropped subjects or verbs.
- Each piece of text passes the stranger test by itself. It does not rely on the title above it, the card around it, or the reader having read the measure.
- Name the subject matter. Say which records, which data, which people or which program the measure is about (for example "Epstein records", "location data"), not only "records" or "data".
- No invented arguments. Give a reason for keeping or changing wording only when a named source actually gave that reason.
- Nothing generic. If a sentence would still be true about a different measure, rewrite it with this measure's names, numbers and events, or cut it.
- Name who does what. The law, the bill, the agency or the official is the grammatical subject. Use the specific name (the Justice Department, the Attorney General), not a bare category word such as "the branch", "the exception", "the rule" or "the report" with nothing saying which one.
- No figures of speech and no idioms. Say what the text does, not what it is like.
- A term quoted from the measure is followed, in the same sentence, by what it refers to.
- A sentence must not appear to contradict itself. If something both must and need not happen, say exactly who must do what and what is left out.
- A question is one complete question that ends with a question mark and names the people, records or agencies involved.
- Describe what the wording allows or leaves out. Do not say whether the policy is good or bad, and do not state the tool's own opinion.
- Do not add, drop or change any fact, number, date, name or source. Rewriting changes only the wording.
- No em dashes or en dashes. No semicolons in plain language.
- Short labels (tags, tab names, pattern names, stakeholder role names, amendment option names) may be noun phrases, but they follow the same rules on plain words and no figures of speech.

## Plain language

- Style: plain statement, written so a seventh grader can follow it. Aim for a reading grade of 7 or lower on every line.
- A title is 8 to 20 words, in one or two short sentences, and states the problem itself. It names the subject (for example the Epstein files) but does not need the measure's full official name.
- Inside an explanation, say "the law", "the bill" or "the resolution" after the subject has been named once. Do not repeat the measure's full name in every sentence.
- Use short, common words: "hide" before "black out" or "redact", "hold back" before "withhold", "marked secret" before "classified", "right to sue" before "cause of action", "must" and "is allowed to" before "duty" and "discretion".
- If a word from the measure matters, quote it and say what it means in everyday terms in the same or the next sentence (for example: The law says the Attorney General "may" hide names. "May" means allowed, not required).
- Say who a person or office is the first time it appears in an explanation (the Attorney General, the country's top law enforcement official).
- No abstract summing-up lines such as "this makes the difference between a duty and a choice". Say the concrete thing that follows.
- Most sentences have 8 to 16 words. None has more than 22. An explanation is two to four sentences.
- Refer to parts of the measure by what they do, never by section number.
- A why-it-matters line says what can happen to a real person or to the public, in one or two short sentences, and does not start with "This matters because".

## Professional

- Style: formal analyst. Complete, neutral sentences in the register of a legislative analysis written for counsel and policy staff.
- A title is one sentence of 10 to 30 words stating the finding, ending with a period.
- Use the precise technical term (redaction, cause of action, savings clause, qualified immunity) without explaining it.
- Cite the section or subsection where the wording appears whenever the source text identifies it.
- State the legal consequence and its basis. Mark anything unsettled or inferred as unsettled or inferred.
- The same facts appear in both versions. Only vocabulary and the level of legal detail differ.

## Examples

**Fails the guide:** "Unclassified" leaves the line with the executive branch

Why: Figure of speech, no actor, quoted term unexplained, does not say what records.

Plain language: The Justice Department does not have to release Epstein records that the government has marked secret, and the government itself decides which records to mark.

Professional: Section 2(a) requires release only of unclassified records, and the authority to classify records rests with the executive branch, which includes the Department of Justice.

**Fails the guide:** Classified records do not have to be released, and the branch that must release them decides what is classified

Why: "The branch" is unnamed, the subject matter is missing, "classified" is unexplained, and it seems to say the records both must and need not be released.

Plain language: The Justice Department does not have to release Epstein records that the government has marked secret, and the government itself decides which records to mark.

Professional: Section 2(a) requires release only of unclassified records, and the authority to classify records rests with the executive branch, which includes the Department of Justice.

**Fails the guide:** Who reviews a decision to keep something secret.

Why: A fragment posing as a sentence. "Something" is unnamed.

Plain language: When the government marks an Epstein record as secret, does anyone outside the government check that decision?

Professional: Should a decision to classify a record covered by the Act be subject to review by a court or an independent body?

**Fails the guide:** The 30-day deadline has nothing behind it

Why: Idiom. Does not say whose deadline or for what.

Plain language: The law gives the Justice Department 30 days to release the Epstein records but sets no consequence if it misses that deadline.

Professional: Section 2(a) sets a 30-day deadline for release of the records, and the Act specifies no consequence for noncompliance.

**Fails the guide:** Permitted, not required.

Why: Fragment with no subject.

Plain language: The law allows the Justice Department to black out victims' names but does not require it.

Professional: Section 2(c)(1) permits, and does not require, redaction of victims' identifying information.

**Fails the guide:** The Epstein Files Transparency Act allows the Attorney General to black out victims' names and personal details in the Epstein records but does not require it.

Why: Too long and too formal for a seventh grader. It repeats the law's full name, and the explanation that follows relies on abstract phrases such as "a duty and a choice" and "no legal duty to point to".

Plain language: The law lets the government hide victims' names in the Epstein files. It does not make the government do it.

Professional: Section 2(c)(1)(A) of the Epstein Files Transparency Act permits, but does not require, the Attorney General to redact victims' identifying information from released Epstein records.
